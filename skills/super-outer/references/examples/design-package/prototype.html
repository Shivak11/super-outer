The Harbour at Low Water

Every morning before six, the first boats leave the inner harbour while the water is still deep enough to clear the sandbar. Their departure times come from the tide table pinned inside the harbour office.

Over the last ten years the sandbar has moved roughly forty metres east. Boats that once left at any state of the tide now wait for the water to rise, and in order to reach the morning market some crews now leave in the dark.

This report follows that change through two seasons of field notes, eleven interviews with people who work on the water, and the harbour board's own records.

What the records show

The board's dredging log shows that the channel was last cleared in full nine years ago. Since then it has been partly cleared twice, each time after a boat ran aground at the harbour mouth.

Each partial clearing removed sand from the deepest part of the channel only. Within a year, the survey soundings were back to where they had been before the work began.

What crews told us

Most of the people we interviewed described the change in hours. A skipper who has fished from the harbour for thirty years said that the tide now decides his whole day, including when he sleeps.

Several crews have started to share a single early boat and split the catch, which means that fewer boats leave the harbour on a typical morning.

The net menders and the ice plant now work to the same tide, so delays at the harbour mouth reach people who never go out on the water.
